Do You Need a Two-Day Colonoscopy Prep if You Are Constipated?

The short answer: Not always, but you may need more than the standard prep. The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force suggests a stronger plan for people at high risk of a poor prep: low-residue food 3 and 2 days before, clear liquids the day before, and a split-dose 4-liter prep plus bisacodyl. Your unit decides which plan you get.

Does constipation really make a colonoscopy prep fail more often?

Yes, by a modest amount. The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF) consensus on bowel preparation lists constipation as a risk factor for an inadequate prep, with an odds ratio of 1.3. It lists opioid use at 1.7 and tricyclic antidepressant use at 2.0. The Task Force says these risk factors may add up.

A 2024 meta-analysis of four trials with 1,927 people found constipation was an independent predictor of inadequate preparation, with an odds ratio of 1.98. A small 2010 New York study of 101 patients found that fewer bowel movements per day went with worse prep scores. Hard stools in women showed the same link.

An inadequate prep matters because the doctor can miss polyps. The USMSTF recommends a repeat colonoscopy within 12 months when a screening or surveillance prep is not good enough. That is the outcome a stronger plan tries to prevent. For what to do on the night itself, see our guide on what to do when colonoscopy prep is not working.

What do US and European guidelines recommend for constipated patients?

The two main guidelines do not agree in detail.

  • US (2025): The USMSTF recommends that people at high risk of a poor prep be managed like people with a past inadequate prep (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence). It then suggests one specific regimen for them (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence). The recommendation was published together in Gastroenterology, The American Journal of Gastroenterology, and Gastrointestinal Endoscopy in April 2025.
  • Europe (2019): The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) 2019 guideline update recommends a low-fiber diet on the day before and a split-dose prep for everyone. Its statement 11, reproduced in the French endoscopy society's bilingual copy, says ESGE "does not suggest any specific bowel preparation in patients with constipation" (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence).

So the US guidance leans toward a stronger plan, and the European guidance stays neutral. Both rest on limited evidence for this group.

Is the "high-risk" plan the same as a two-day prep?

Not exactly. Many people hear "two-day prep" and picture two full days of laxative drinks. The USMSTF plan is different. It stretches the diet changes over three days, but the main laxative is still a split dose taken the evening before and the morning of the exam.

Some units do use two-day purgative protocols. We did not find an adult trial in constipated patients that tested two full days of prep drink against a standard split dose. If your unit gives you a two-day plan, it is a local protocol. Follow it as written.

Does a longer low-residue diet help if you are constipated?

For most people, no. The USMSTF found that extra days of diet changes "confer no benefit" over one day for people at low risk. The 2024 meta-analysis found 1-day and 3-day low-residue diets gave similar results, and the 1-day diet was easier to tolerate.

Constipation is the exception that has not been settled. The authors of that meta-analysis wrote that the right diet length for constipated patients "still needs further clinical trials." The USMSTF high-risk plan uses a low-residue diet on days 3 and 2 before the exam anyway. It bases this on data from people who repeated a colonoscopy after a failed prep. For foods that fit, see what to eat the week before a colonoscopy.

Do extra laxatives in the days before help?

The trials in constipated patients give mixed results.

  • Intensive regimens overall: A 2023 network meta-analysis of 11 trials with 1,891 constipated patients found that intensive regimens raised the odds of an adequate prep (odds ratio 2.19). The trials differed a lot from each other.
  • Bisacodyl plus a smaller prep: An Italian trial of 400 adults with chronic constipation compared 2 liters of PEG plus 2 days of bisacodyl with 4 liters of PEG. Cleansing success was 80.2% versus 81.4%, so neither was better. Patients found the 2-liter plan easier to take.
  • Lubiprostone: A Thai trial of 78 constipated patients found no added benefit from lubiprostone (50% versus 52.9% adequate).
  • Linaclotide: A Chinese trial of 319 constipated patients found linaclotide for 3 days plus 3 liters of PEG beat 4 liters of PEG alone (89.4% versus 73.6% adequate). It was a single-center study.

None of these adjuncts is a standard US recommendation for constipation. Do not add laxatives on your own. Some prep labels warn against it, so ask your unit first.

What could a high-risk prep timeline look like?

This timeline lays out the USMSTF suggestions for people at high risk. It is not a substitute for your unit's sheet. Your doctor may change any step.

  1. When you book (1 to 2 weeks before): Tell the unit about chronic constipation, opioid or other constipating medicines, and any past poor prep. The USMSTF recommends choosing a prep based on your history, medicines, and past prep results. Ask if the unit has a high-risk protocol.
  2. About a week before: Ask your prescriber whether to pause any constipating medicine. The USMSTF lists a temporary stop of anticholinergic, opioid, or other constipating medicines as an option after a failed prep. Never stop an opioid without your prescriber. Our guide to medicines to stop before a colonoscopy covers the common ones.
  3. Day 3 and day 2 before: Eat a low-residue, low-fiber diet. The USMSTF also lists limiting vegetables and legumes for 2 to 3 days as an option.
  4. Day 1 before: Clear liquids only, all day. Take the bisacodyl dose in the afternoon if your unit prescribes it. Start the first half of the split-dose prep in the evening.
  5. Exam day: Take the second half. ESGE recommends starting the last dose within 5 hours of the exam and finishing at least 2 hours before it starts.

How do you raise constipation with your endoscopy unit?

Bring it up when you book, not the night before. Give plain facts: how many bowel movements you have each week, whether stools are hard, and any laxatives you use. List every medicine. The NIDDK lists narcotic pain medicines, iron supplements, calcium channel blockers, and some antidepressants among those that worsen constipation.

The NIDDK also lists hormone conditions such as hypothyroidism as a cause of constipation. If your constipation is new or getting worse, ask about a thyroid check. Our sister site thyroid.md explains hypothyroidism.

Then ask three things. Does the unit flag constipation as high risk? Will you get a longer diet or an added laxative? Who do you call if the prep does not start working?

The bottom line

Constipation and opioid use raise the risk of a poor prep. The 2025 USMSTF suggests a stronger plan for high-risk patients: low-residue food 3 and 2 days before, clear liquids the day before, and a split-dose 4-liter prep plus bisacodyl. The evidence is weak, and the 2019 ESGE guideline does not single out constipation. Tell your unit early and follow its instructions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I take MiraLAX for a few days before my colonoscopy if I am constipated?

Only if your endoscopy unit tells you to. Some units add a laxative in the days before for constipated patients, but no US guideline makes this a standard step. Ask when you book so the plan fits your prep.

Do opioids make colonoscopy prep harder?

Yes. The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force lists opioid use as a risk factor for an inadequate prep, with an odds ratio of 1.7. Tell your unit and your prescriber about any opioid before your exam. Do not stop it on your own.

Is a 4-liter prep better than a 2-liter prep for constipation?

Not clearly. In an Italian trial of 400 constipated adults, 2 liters of PEG plus bisacodyl cleaned the bowel about as well as 4 liters of PEG. The 2025 US plan for high-risk patients still names a split-dose 4-liter prep.

Will an enema before the prep help if I am constipated?

One Turkish trial of 227 patients found that an enema before the prep drink improved right colon cleansing, mostly in women with more constipation. It is a single study, and enemas are not a guideline step. Ask your unit before you use one.

Should I tell the unit about constipation even if my last prep went fine?

Yes. The Task Force recommends choosing a prep based on your history, medicines, and past prep results. A good past prep is useful information, and the unit may keep you on the standard plan.
