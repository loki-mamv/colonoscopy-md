How Often Does a Colonoscopy Miss Polyps or Cancer?

October 16, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Colonoscopy misses more small polyps than most people expect, but it rarely misses cancer. A 2019 meta-analysis of more than 15,000 tandem exams found a 26% miss rate for adenomas, but only 9% for advanced adenomas. Across Western countries, about 7.5% of colorectal cancers show up within 3 years after a colonoscopy that did not find them. Good bowel prep and a skilled, careful endoscopist lower both numbers.

How do researchers measure missed polyps?

The main tool is the tandem colonoscopy. A patient has two colonoscopies back to back, often on the same day. Any polyp found on the second pass that the first pass did not find counts as a miss.

The largest pooled analysis of these studies appeared in Gastroenterology in 2019. It combined 43 publications and more than 15,000 tandem colonoscopies. The pooled miss rates were:

  • Adenomas (any size): 26%.
  • Advanced adenomas: 9%. These are the larger or more abnormal polyps most likely to turn into cancer.
  • Serrated polyps: 27%.
  • Flat adenomas: 34%.
  • Patients at high risk for colorectal cancer: 33%.

These are per-polyp numbers, not per-patient numbers. A 26% miss rate does not mean one in four people walks out with an undetected cancer. Most missed adenomas are small, and most small adenomas never become cancer. The same analysis found that adequate bowel prep and add-on techniques were linked to lower miss rates.

How often is cancer found after a clear colonoscopy?

A cancer found after a colonoscopy that did not detect it is called a post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer, or PCCRC. The World Endoscopy Organization created a standard way to measure it in 2018. The key figure is the 3-year rate: of all colorectal cancers diagnosed, what share were found within 3 years of a colonoscopy that missed them?

A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 8 studies that followed this method. They came from 4 Western European and 2 North American countries and included 220,106 colorectal cancers. The pooled 3-year PCCRC rate was 7.5%. The rate fell over time, from 7.9% in 2006 to 6.7% in 2012.

That 7.5% describes cancers, not patients. It says that about 1 in 13 colorectal cancers had a recent colonoscopy that missed it. For a single person with a normal exam, the chance of cancer in the next few years is far lower.

A 2010 modeling study estimated about 1.8 cancers per 1,000 screened people within 5 years from missed lesions. The range ran from 0.5 to 3.5 per 1,000, depending on the assumptions used.

Why do cancers appear after a colonoscopy?

The same 2010 study lists three ways a cancer can follow a colonoscopy:

  1. A missed lesion. A polyp or early cancer was there but not seen.
  2. Incomplete removal. A polyp was removed, but some tissue stayed behind and grew back.
  3. A new lesion. A fast-growing polyp formed after the exam.

Its model suggested that most of these cancers likely reflect missed lesions rather than new ones. The 2025 meta-analysis found which groups carry higher PCCRC rates:

  • People with inflammatory bowel disease: 29.3%.
  • People with a prior colorectal cancer: 29.8%.
  • People with diverticular disease: 11.6%.
  • Cancers on the right (proximal) side of the colon: 8.6%.
  • Women: 7.9%.

If a doctor could not reach the end of the colon, the exam was incomplete. Our guide on what happens after an incomplete colonoscopy explains the usual next steps.

Does the doctor performing the colonoscopy matter?

Yes. The best-known measure is the adenoma detection rate (ADR). ADR is the share of a doctor's screening colonoscopies that find at least one adenoma.

A 2014 Kaiser Permanente study in the New England Journal of Medicine looked at 314,872 colonoscopies by 136 gastroenterologists. Their ADRs ranged from 7.4% to 52.5%. Patients of doctors in the top fifth for ADR had about half the risk of interval cancer of patients in the bottom fifth (hazard ratio 0.52). Their risk of a fatal interval cancer was also lower (hazard ratio 0.38). Each 1% rise in ADR was linked to a 3% drop in cancer risk.

The 2024 ACG/ASGE quality indicators for colonoscopy set new targets. They include:

  • An ADR of at least 35% (40% in men and 30% in women).
  • Reaching the cecum, the start of the colon, in at least 95% of exams.
  • An average withdrawal time of at least 8 minutes in normal exams.
  • A sessile serrated lesion detection rate of at least 6%.

You can ask your endoscopist about their ADR. Many centers track it.

How much does bowel prep affect what gets missed?

A lot. Stool left in the colon can hide polyps, especially flat ones. The 2024 quality indicators list adequate bowel prep as a priority measure. They set a target that at least 90% of a center's exams have adequate prep.

A 2012 study in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy followed 133 patients who had a poor prep on screening and later had a repeat exam. The per-adenoma miss rate was 47.9%. On the repeat exam, 33.8% had at least 1 adenoma. Among patients with adenomas on the repeat, 31.1% had shown no polyps on the first exam.

This is why a poor prep often leads to a shorter time before the next exam. If yours did not go well, see our guide on what to do if colonoscopy prep is not working.

Why are right-sided and flat polyps harder to find?

The right side of the colon is the farthest from the entry point. It has deep folds, and prep there is often less clean.

Polyp shape matters too. The National Cancer Institute notes that flat or hard-to-see lesions include serrated polyps. These may be more common in the right colon than the left. Sessile serrated lesions are often pale, flat, and covered with mucus, so they blend into the colon wall.

The tandem data reflect this. Proximal advanced adenomas had a 14% miss rate, compared with 9% for advanced adenomas overall. Flat adenomas had a 34% miss rate. That is why the 2024 quality indicators added a separate detection target for sessile serrated lesions.

What can you do to lower your risk of a miss?

Most of the miss risk sits with the procedure, but you control some of it.

  • Do the prep fully. Follow the timing in your written instructions and finish every dose.
  • Ask about quality. Ask your doctor for their ADR and whether they photo-document reaching the cecum.
  • Read your report. Check whether the prep was rated adequate and whether the exam reached the cecum.
  • Keep to your follow-up interval. Intervals depend on what was found. See our guide to colonoscopy intervals after polyps.
  • Report new symptoms. Rectal bleeding, a change in bowel habits, or anemia should not wait for your next scheduled exam.

The bottom line

Colonoscopy misses about 1 in 4 adenomas in tandem studies, but only about 1 in 11 advanced adenomas. About 7.5% of colorectal cancers in Western countries are found within 3 years of a colonoscopy that missed them. A clean prep and a high-ADR endoscopist are the two factors most tied to fewer misses. Colonoscopy is still the most complete look at the colon, and a normal exam remains strong reassurance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 26% adenoma miss rate a reason to skip colonoscopy?

No. The 26% figure counts individual polyps, and most missed polyps are small adenomas that rarely become cancer. The miss rate for advanced adenomas was 9%. Colonoscopy still finds and removes most precancerous growths in a single exam.

What is an interval cancer?

An interval cancer is a colorectal cancer found before the next recommended exam after a colonoscopy that did not find it. The World Endoscopy Organization now uses the broader term post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer. Researchers usually count cancers diagnosed within 3 years of the exam.

Can I find out my doctor's adenoma detection rate?

Often, yes. Many endoscopy centers track it because it is a national quality indicator. The 2024 ACG and ASGE target is at least 35% overall. You can ask the doctor or the center before you book.

Does a poor bowel prep mean I need a repeat colonoscopy?

Often it does. In one study of patients with inadequate prep, the per-adenoma miss rate was about 48%. Your doctor will usually recommend a shorter interval or a repeat exam based on the prep rating in your report.

Are some people at higher risk of a missed cancer?

Yes. A 2025 meta-analysis found much higher post-colonoscopy cancer rates in people with inflammatory bowel disease or a prior colorectal cancer. Rates were also higher with diverticular disease and for right-sided cancers. These patients often follow closer surveillance schedules.
