Is It Normal to Have Pain or Bloating After a Colonoscopy?

September 15, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Yes. Mild cramping, bloating, and lots of gas are the most common after-effects of a colonoscopy. They come from the gas used to open up the colon during the exam, and they usually fade within hours. Pain that is severe, keeps getting worse, or comes with fever is not normal and needs a call to your doctor right away.

Why does a colonoscopy leave you gassy and crampy?

A collapsed colon is hard to see inside. To get a clear view, the doctor pumps gas through the scope to gently inflate it. MedlinePlus notes that either air or carbon dioxide is used for this step. Some of that gas stays behind when the scope comes out.

The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) reviewed colonoscopy side effects in 2019. Its review of adverse events names three likely causes of discomfort afterward:

  • Gas distention: the stretched colon feels full and tight until the gas moves out.
  • Colonic spasm: the bowel muscle can cramp as it reacts to the exam.
  • Mechanical pressure: the scope pushes on the bowel wall as it moves around bends, especially when it loops.

These symptoms are common. ASGE lists bloating in 2.6% to 25% of patients and abdominal pain or discomfort in 2.5% to 11%, depending on the study. The wide range reflects differences in technique, sedation, and how each study asked patients about symptoms.

Sedation usually means you feel none of this during the exam itself. If you are curious how the experience changes with lighter sedation, see our guide on whether you can be awake for a colonoscopy.

Does carbon dioxide cause less pain than air?

Yes, and the evidence is fairly strong. Carbon dioxide (CO2) is rapidly absorbed from the colon and breathed out through the lungs, so less gas stays trapped after the exam.

Randomized trials comparing the two gases point the same way:

  • A Norwegian randomized trial of 240 unsedated patients (Gut, 2002) found less pain after the exam with CO2. It also found no rise in exhaled CO2 levels, a key safety check.
  • A 2015 meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials with 3,607 patients found less pain during the procedure and less pain at 1, 6, and 24 hours afterward with CO2.
  • A 2016 meta-analysis of 24 randomized trials with 3,996 patients found CO2 reduced pain during and after the procedure for up to 24 hours. Completion rates and exam times were similar with both gases.

The 2019 ASGE review cites this work and agrees that CO2 produces less post-procedure pain than standard air. Water-based techniques, which fill the colon with water instead of gas, may also reduce pain, especially with light or no sedation.

Air is still used in many settings. If bloating bothered you after a past colonoscopy, it is reasonable to ask which gas your center uses.

How long does normal bloating and cramping last?

For most people, not long. NIDDK, part of the National Institutes of Health, says you may feel cramping or bloating during the first hour after the procedure. MedlinePlus describes mild cramping, passing a lot of gas, feeling bloated, and sometimes feeling sick to your stomach, and says these feelings soon go away.

Some people notice mild gas pains longer, into the evening or the next day. The CO2 trials above measured pain out to 24 hours, and air-insufflation groups still reported more pain at that point. The ASGE review calls gas-related discomfort usually self-limited, meaning it clears on its own and rarely needs a hospital stay.

A useful pattern to watch: normal gas pain is crampy, comes and goes, and eases each time you pass gas. Over the hours after the exam it should trend better, not worse.

What helps relieve gas pain after the procedure?

The main fix is simply letting the gas out. MedlinePlus says passing gas is necessary and should be expected,. Holding it back only prolongs the discomfort.

  • Pass gas freely. The gas has to leave before the fullness eases.
  • Follow your discharge sheet. MedlinePlus lists drinking plenty of liquids and eating a healthy meal among common home instructions.
  • Ask before taking medicine. If polyps were removed, your team may want you to avoid some pain relievers for a period. Check your instructions first.
  • Plan ahead next time. Ask whether your center uses CO2, which reduced pain in the trials above.

Food, activity, and sedation recovery are covered in our colonoscopy recovery tips. The point here is narrower: ordinary gas pain gets better as the gas leaves. If it does not, the cause may be something else.

Which kinds of pain point to a complication?

Serious problems are uncommon, but pain is the symptom that can signal them. Three are worth knowing.

Perforation (a tear in the colon wall). In ASGE's pooled analysis of more than 10 million colonoscopies, perforation occurred in 5.8 per 10,000 exams. NIDDK gives a lower estimate of about 3 per 10,000. Warning signs include severe or worsening belly pain and fever. ASGE notes that some tears let air escape upward, causing unusual signs such as chest pain, neck swelling, or shortness of breath.

Post-polypectomy electrocoagulation syndrome. This can follow removal of a polyp with an electric current. The heat injures the full thickness of the bowel wall and irritates the lining of the abdomen, but without a hole. According to ASGE, it typically appears 1 to 5 days after the exam with fever, pain in one spot, and a high white blood cell count. Reported rates vary widely, from about 1 in 100 down to 3 per 100,000. It is much more common, 7% to 8%, after endoscopic submucosal dissection, an advanced technique for large lesions. Most cases are treated in the hospital with IV fluids, antibiotics, and bowel rest. Learn more about why polyps are removed.

Bleeding. ASGE puts bleeding at 2.4 per 1,000 colonoscopies, and most cases follow polyp removal. Heavy bleeding or bleeding that does not stop is a red flag.

NIDDK advises getting care right away for any of these:

  • Severe pain in your abdomen
  • Fever
  • Bloody bowel movements that do not get better, or bleeding from the anus that does not stop
  • Dizziness or weakness

Timing matters too. Pain that starts or worsens a day or more after the exam, rather than fading, deserves a call even if it seems modest. If you are unsure whether a new symptom fits, a tool like symptom.md can help you think through it, but call your endoscopy team first.

The bottom line

Mild cramping, bloating, and gas are normal after a colonoscopy. They come from the gas used to inflate the colon and usually ease within hours. Randomized trials show carbon dioxide causes less post-procedure pain than air. Severe or worsening pain, fever, or ongoing bleeding is not normal and needs prompt medical attention.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I feel sick to my stomach after a colonoscopy?

MedlinePlus lists feeling bloated and sick to your stomach among the expected after-effects, and says these feelings soon go away. Sedation medicines and a colon stretched by gas both play a part. Vomiting that keeps going, or nausea with severe pain or fever, should be reported to your doctor.

Can a colonoscopy cause pain a few days later?

Ordinary gas pain should improve, not start fresh days later. Post-polypectomy electrocoagulation syndrome typically appears 1 to 5 days after the exam with fever and pain in one spot, according to ASGE. Any new or worsening pain after the first day deserves a call to your endoscopy team.

Is carbon dioxide safe to use during a colonoscopy?

Randomized trials have found it safe. A Norwegian trial of 240 unsedated patients found no rise in exhaled carbon dioxide levels, and a 2016 meta-analysis of 24 trials reported no adverse effect from CO2 retention. It is absorbed and breathed out faster than air.

How rare is a perforated colon from colonoscopy?

It is uncommon. ASGE's pooled analysis of more than 10 million exams found 5.8 perforations per 10,000 colonoscopies, and NIDDK estimates about 3 per 10,000. Risk is higher with some conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease, and with advanced polyp removal techniques.

Does having polyps removed make bloating worse?

The main cause of bloating is the gas used to inflate the colon, which happens whether or not polyps are removed. Polyp removal does raise the chance of bleeding and, rarely, electrocoagulation syndrome. Follow any extra instructions your team gives you after polypectomy.
