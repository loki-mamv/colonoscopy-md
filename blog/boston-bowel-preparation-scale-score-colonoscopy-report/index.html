What Does a Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) Score Mean on a Colonoscopy Report?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: The Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) scores how clean each of 3 colon segments was, from 0 to 3, for a total of 0 to 9. Per the 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force recommendations, a prep is adequate only when every segment scores 2 or higher. Any segment below 2 means a repeat colonoscopy within 12 months.

What is the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale?

The BBPS is a standard way for the doctor to record how well they could see the lining of your colon. Gastroenterologists at Boston University Medical Center built it and tested it in 633 screening colonoscopies. They published it in 2009 in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (Lai et al.).

The scale replaced vague words like "excellent," "good," "fair," and "poor." Those words meant different things to different doctors. The BBPS uses fixed descriptions instead, so two doctors who look at the same colon should give it about the same score.

One detail matters a lot when you read your report. The doctor gives the score after washing and suctioning out any leftover liquid or stool. So the score measures what the doctor could actually see, not how the colon looked on first entry. The 2025 Task Force recommends this timing for every prep rating.

How is each colon segment scored from 0 to 3?

The BBPS splits the colon into 3 segments. Per the original paper, they are:

  • Right colon: the cecum and ascending colon.
  • Transverse colon: including the hepatic and splenic flexures (the two bends).
  • Left colon: the descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum.

Each segment then gets one of 4 scores:

  • 0: unprepared. Solid stool that cannot be cleared hides the lining.
  • 1: the doctor sees part of the lining, but staining, leftover stool, or cloudy liquid hides other areas.
  • 2: small amounts of staining, small bits of stool, or cloudy liquid remain, but the lining is seen well.
  • 3: the whole lining is seen well, with no staining, stool fragments, or cloudy liquid.

If the doctor stops the exam early because of poor prep, any segment they did not reach gets a 0. Your report may list the 3 numbers separately (for example "BBPS 3-2-3") or give only the total.

What does the total score from 0 to 9 mean?

The 3 segment scores add up to a total from 0 to 9. A 9 means a fully clean colon. A 0 means an unprepared colon. In the 2009 validation study, the average total was 6.0. Exams with a total of 5 or more found polyps more often than exams below 5 (40% vs 24%).

The total alone can mislead, though. A 2014 study of 2,516 normal screening exams by 74 endoscopists, Calderwood et al., looked at what doctors actually recommended:

  • When every segment scored 2 or more, doctors advised the next exam in 10 years in 90% of cases.
  • When the total was 0 to 2, doctors advised a repeat within 1 year in 96% of cases.
  • Totals of 3 to 5 got mixed advice. The authors called for a consensus on this middle zone.

Current guidance settled that question by focusing on the segments, not the total.

What BBPS score counts as adequate prep?

Two current documents agree on the cutoff. The 2024 ACG/ASGE quality indicators for colonoscopy define adequate prep as a BBPS score of 2 or more in each of the 3 segments. They prefer the BBPS over other scales because its scores come with explicit descriptions.

The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force update (also published in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy) goes one step further. It defines "adequate" as a prep that lets the doctor assign the standard screening or surveillance interval. It supports treating a total below 6, or any segment below 2, as inadequate.

That means a total of 6 or more is not enough by itself. A 3-3-1 adds up to 7, but the right colon scored 1. That exam is still inadequate. The Task Force also says a report that uses the word "fair" should state plainly whether the prep was adequate.

Both groups want at least 90% of a doctor's exams to have adequate prep. That is a quality target for each doctor and each endoscopy unit, and you can ask your doctor for their rate.

How do you read a score on your own report? Worked examples

Here is how the 2025 Task Force rule applies to some common report lines. The segments are listed right, transverse, left.

  1. BBPS 3-3-3 (total 9): adequate. Your next exam follows the normal schedule for your findings.
  2. BBPS 2-2-2 (total 6): adequate. Every segment met the cutoff, even though small bits of stool or staining were present.
  3. BBPS 2-3-3 (total 8): adequate. A 2 in the right colon is still enough.
  4. BBPS 1-3-3 (total 7): inadequate. The right colon scored below 2, so polyps there could have been missed.
  5. BBPS 1-2-2 (total 5): inadequate. Expect advice to repeat the exam within 12 months.
  6. BBPS 0-0-2 (total 2): inadequate. This often means the exam was stopped early. Our page on an incomplete colonoscopy covers that case.

If your report gives only a total, ask for the 3 segment scores. A total of 6 or 7 can hide one weak segment.

What happens if your prep was inadequate?

A poor prep lowers the chance of finding polyps. The 2025 Task Force cites a meta-analysis of 11 studies and more than 55,000 colonoscopies. Inadequate prep had a 5% lower absolute adenoma detection rate. In one study it cites, repeat exams after an inadequate prep found 42% of adenomas that the first exam missed. For more on miss rates, see how often a colonoscopy misses polyps or cancer.

Here is what the 2025 Task Force recommends:

  1. Same-day rescue, if possible. The doctor may try more washing, an enema, or extra prep drink with a repeat exam later that day or the next day.
  2. Repeat colonoscopy within 12 months. For screening or surveillance exams, it recommends completing a colonoscopy within 12 months. It says this should happen as soon as feasible.
  3. A changed prep plan next time. Options include clearer instructions, patient navigation, avoiding vegetables and legumes for 2 to 3 days, clear liquids only the day before, treating constipation, pausing constipating medicines, or a higher-volume prep.

People with one inadequate prep have a high chance of a second one, so the plan for the repeat exam matters. Do not change your medicines on your own. Ask the doctor who ordered the prep. Our guide to a prep that is not working covers what to do on prep night.

If you have new symptoms while you wait, such as rectal bleeding or a change in bowel habits, do not wait for the repeat date. symptom.md can help you sort out which symptoms need a call to your doctor.

The bottom line

The BBPS gives each of 3 colon segments a score from 0 to 3, and the total runs from 0 to 9. Read the segment scores, not just the total. Under the 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force and 2024 ACG/ASGE guidance, adequate means 2 or higher in every segment. If any segment scored 0 or 1, expect a repeat colonoscopy within 12 months with a changed prep plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a BBPS score of 6 good?

It depends on how the 6 is split. A 2-2-2 is adequate because every segment reached 2. A 3-2-1 also totals 6 but is inadequate, because one segment scored 1.

Is a BBPS of 9 required for a normal 10-year interval?

No. A perfect 9 is not needed. Current ACG/ASGE and US Multi-Society Task Force guidance treats a score of 2 or more in every segment as adequate, so standard intervals can apply.

Why would one segment score lower than the others?

Each segment is scored on its own, so one part of the colon can be less clean than the rest. The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force recommends choosing your next prep with past prep results in mind. Bring your report to your next pre-colonoscopy visit.

My report says prep was fair. Is that the same as a BBPS score?

No. Fair is an older descriptive term without a fixed definition. The 2025 US Multi-Society Task Force says a report that uses fair should also state whether the prep was adequate, so ask your doctor if it is not clear.

Does an inadequate prep mean the colonoscopy found nothing useful?

Not always. Polyps seen in clean segments can still be removed and sent for testing. The concern is the areas the doctor could not see well, which is why a repeat exam is advised.
