What Does Internal Hemorrhoids Mean on a Colonoscopy Report?

The short answer: "Internal hemorrhoids" means the doctor saw swollen veins in the lining of your lower rectum and anal canal. It is one of the most common side findings on a colonoscopy. In a 2012 screening study of 976 adults, 38.9% had hemorrhoids, and most were grade I. Without symptoms, they usually need no treatment.

What did the doctor actually see?

Hemorrhoids are veins, not growths. The NIDDK defines them as swollen, inflamed veins around the anus or in the lower rectum. Internal hemorrhoids form in the lining of the anus and lower rectum. External hemorrhoids form under the skin around the anus.

A 2025 review in JAMA adds the anatomy. Internal hemorrhoids start above the dentate line, the boundary between the upper and lower anal canal. External hemorrhoids arise below it. That is why a scope sees internal hemorrhoids from the inside, while external ones are seen or felt on the skin.

The finding is often unplanned. The NIDDK diagnosis page notes that doctors may find internal hemorrhoids during a colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy done for other reasons. In plain terms, the scope was looking for polyps and saw these veins on the way in or out.

How common is this finding?

Very common. A few numbers from sources we read:

  • The NIDDK says hemorrhoids affect about 1 in 20 Americans, and about half of adults older than 50 have them.
  • The American College of Gastroenterology patient page says nearly half of Americans have hemorrhoids by age 50.
  • In an Austrian screening colonoscopy study of 976 adults, 380 people (38.93%) had hemorrhoids.
  • In that same study, 55.26% of the people with hemorrhoids reported no symptoms at all.

So if you never noticed a problem, that fits the data. The ACG page says the same thing in plain words: many people have hemorrhoids but no symptoms.

What do grades and words like "small" or "non-bleeding" mean?

Reports use a few kinds of modifiers. Here is how to read them.

  1. Grade I to IV. The 2025 JAMA review grades internal hemorrhoids by prolapse. Grade I bulges into the anal canal. Grade II comes out beyond the anus and goes back by itself. Grade III needs to be pushed back by hand. Grade IV stays out and cannot be pushed back.
  2. Which grades are common. In the Austrian screening study, 72.89% of hemorrhoids were grade I and 18.42% were grade II. Only 8.16% were grade III, and 0.53% were grade IV.
  3. Small, medium, or large. These words describe how big the veins looked to the endoscopist. They are a visual description, not a grade. Ask your doctor whether the size matters for you.
  4. Non-bleeding. This describes what the doctor saw during the exam: no active bleeding from the veins at that time. It does not mean they never bleed.

A grade on a sedated, relaxed patient may differ from what happens when you strain at home. Your own history of prolapse or bleeding matters as much as the report.

Do internal hemorrhoids need treatment?

Usually not, if they cause no symptoms. Treatment is aimed at symptoms such as bleeding, prolapse, or itching. The ACG says most hemorrhoids get better on their own or with simple measures.

If you do have symptoms, the 2024 American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) guideline sets the order of care. The American Family Physician summary of that guideline (December 2025) lists these points:

  • Check for constipation first, because treating it can cut symptoms a lot.
  • Increase fiber and fluids. A Cochrane review of about 380 patients found fiber cut persistent symptoms by 53% compared with usual care.
  • Avoid straining and long spells on the toilet.
  • Office procedures such as rubber band ligation, injection sclerotherapy, and infrared coagulation are preferred over surgery.

The JAMA review reports that rubber band ligation resolves symptoms in 89% of patients, though up to 20% need repeat banding. Surgery is mainly for grade III to IV disease that does not improve with less invasive care. The 2021 ACG clinical guideline on benign anorectal disorders also covers hemorrhoid care for your doctor's reference.

Do hemorrhoids explain rectal bleeding?

Often, but you should not assume it. The NIDDK says internal hemorrhoids can cause bright red blood on the stool, on toilet paper, or in the bowl. Internal hemorrhoids that do not prolapse are most often painless. The ACG notes that a few drops of blood can turn the toilet water a dramatic color.

The same NIDDK page warns that rectal bleeding can also come from Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, or colorectal cancer. The ASCRS guideline summary is direct about the risk: bleeding from colon cancer is often blamed on hemorrhoids.

This is where your report helps. If you had bleeding and the colonoscopy reached the whole colon with good prep, the doctor has looked for those other causes. Check your prep score, explained in our guide to the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale. The AAFP summary says colonoscopy should be considered if bleeding continues after successful hemorrhoid treatment. For a general look at warning signs, symptom.md covers symptoms by body area.

Does it change when you need your next colonoscopy?

Not on its own. The 2020 US Multi-Society Task Force update on follow-up is built around colonoscopy and polypectomy results, meaning the polyps found and removed. Hemorrhoids are veins, not polyps. Your interval depends on polyp findings and prep quality, so ask your doctor to state the date on your report.

Hemorrhoids often appear next to other side findings, such as diverticulosis on a colonoscopy report. Neither one is a polyp.

If you know you have hemorrhoids, your next prep may sting. Our post on colonoscopy prep with hemorrhoids or a sore bottom covers skin protection.

When should you call the doctor?

Ask at your follow-up visit if any of these apply:

  • You have bleeding from the rectum. The NIDDK lists this as a reason to see a doctor.
  • You still have symptoms after 1 week of home treatment, per the same NIDDK page.
  • Tissue comes out when you have a bowel movement, or you must push it back.
  • Your report gives a grade III or IV, and you want to discuss procedures.
  • You have bleeding plus abdominal pain, new or worse constipation, or bleeding that continues after treatment. The ASCRS summary names these as reasons to consider colonoscopy.

Seek care right away for severe anal pain with rectal bleeding, especially with belly pain, diarrhea, or fever. That warning comes from the NIDDK treatment page.

The bottom line

"Internal hemorrhoids" on a colonoscopy report means the doctor saw swollen veins inside the lower rectum and anal canal. It is common, often found by chance, and often causes no symptoms. Grades I to IV describe prolapse, and most screening findings are grade I. Treat symptoms, not the report line. Do not let the finding explain away new or ongoing bleeding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are internal hemorrhoids on a colonoscopy report a sign of cancer?

No. Hemorrhoids are swollen veins, not polyps or tumors. The concern is different: bleeding from colon cancer can be wrongly blamed on hemorrhoids. A complete colonoscopy with good prep is the test that looks for those other causes.

Why did no one tell me I had hemorrhoids before?

Many people with hemorrhoids have no symptoms. In one screening study, more than half of the people found to have hemorrhoids reported none. A colonoscopy simply gives the doctor a close look at the lower rectum.

Did the colonoscopy cause my hemorrhoids?

There is no evidence in the sources we read that the scope creates hemorrhoids. They are linked to straining, constipation or diarrhea, pregnancy, heavy lifting, and aging. The colonoscopy most likely found hemorrhoids that were already there.

Can grade I internal hemorrhoids go away?

Symptoms often settle with fiber, fluids, and less straining, and many people never have symptoms at all. The ACG notes that hemorrhoids commonly come back, with quiet periods in between. Ask your doctor whether anything needs follow-up.

Should I see a surgeon for hemorrhoids found on colonoscopy?

Usually only if they cause symptoms that do not improve with diet and home care. Office procedures such as rubber band ligation come before surgery in the 2024 ASCRS guideline. Surgery is mainly for grade III to IV disease that does not respond.
