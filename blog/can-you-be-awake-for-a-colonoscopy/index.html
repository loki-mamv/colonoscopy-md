Can You Be Awake for a Colonoscopy?

Most Americans assume a colonoscopy means being knocked out. The consent form mentions sedation, the nurse asks who is driving you home, and no one presents it as a choice. It is a choice.

The short answer

Yes, you can be awake for a colonoscopy. Sedation is standard practice in the United States, but it is not medically required for most people. In a community study of 2,091 patients offered the option, 27.6% chose to start without sedation, and 81.1% of them finished the entire exam without any medication. Of those who finished unsedated, 97.4% said they were satisfied and would do it the same way again.

What are the sedation options for a colonoscopy?

Sedation is a spectrum, not a switch. The American Society of Anesthesiologists defines four levels:

  • No sedation or minimal sedation: you are fully awake, or relaxed but responding normally to conversation.
  • Moderate sedation (often called "conscious sedation" or "twilight"): you are drowsy but respond purposefully to voice. This is the classic combination of midazolam, a benzodiazepine, plus an opioid such as fentanyl. Many people remember little or nothing afterward.
  • Deep sedation: usually propofol, often given by an anesthesia professional. You cannot be easily roused and typically remember nothing.
  • General anesthesia: full loss of consciousness. This is uncommon for routine screening colonoscopy.

The 2018 ASGE sedation guideline calls the opioid-plus-benzodiazepine combination safe and effective for minimal to moderate sedation in patients without special risk factors. It suggests propofol when it is expected to improve comfort, safety, or procedure completion. Which level you get often depends on your endoscopy center's staffing and habits, not on anything about you.

How common is staying awake in the United States?

Rare, and the trend has moved toward deeper sedation, not lighter. A JAMA analysis of insurance claims found that the share of outpatient endoscopies and colonoscopies using a separate anesthesia provider roughly doubled between 2003 and 2009: from 13.5% to 30.2% among Medicare patients, and from 13.6% to 35.5% among commercially insured patients. That mostly reflects the growth of propofol deep sedation.

At the other end of the spectrum, one U.S. center's database review presented at the American College of Gastroenterology 2020 meeting found that only 0.7% of its colonoscopies were performed unsedated. So awake colonoscopy is fully possible, but in the U.S. it is the exception.

That is a cultural pattern more than a clinical one. In many European and Asian countries, colonoscopy with light or no sedation is routine. The Petrini study quoted above showed that when a typical American patient population was simply offered the choice, more than a quarter took it.

What does an unsedated colonoscopy feel like?

Not nothing, but usually not sharp pain either. The colon has no pain receptors for cutting or biopsy, which is why polyp removal itself does not hurt. What you feel is pressure and cramping, mostly from air or water used to open the colon and from the scope stretching the bowel as it rounds two sharp bends. The insertion phase, typically the first 10 to 15 minutes, is the uncomfortable part. The withdrawal phase, when the doctor actually inspects, is usually easy.

Technique matters a lot. In randomized trials, water-exchange insertion, where the endoscopist infuses and suctions water instead of inflating the colon with gas, reduced real-time insertion pain and improved completion of unsedated colonoscopy compared with water immersion. A multicenter European trial found patients randomized to water exchange were significantly more likely to report no pain at all than those examined with standard CO2 insufflation. If you want an awake exam, an endoscopist who uses a water-assisted technique stacks the odds in your favor.

Why would anyone skip sedation?

Several practical reasons come up again and again:

  • You can get on with your day. After sedation, the NIDDK notes you will stay in recovery for about 30 to 60 minutes, cannot drive afterward, and must have a prearranged ride home. An unsedated patient can usually leave shortly after the exam, and many centers allow you to drive yourself. Confirm your center's policy first, because some require an escort for everyone.
  • No escort problem. People without a friend or family member available on a weekday often delay screening. Skipping sedation can remove that barrier.
  • Avoiding sedation risks. Cardiopulmonary events related to sedation are among the more common colonoscopy complications, and skipping sedation avoids them entirely. This matters most for people with sleep apnea or heart or lung disease, though those same people should make the decision with their doctor.
  • Cost. A separate anesthesia professional generates a separate bill. Our guide to what a colonoscopy costs in 2026 covers where those charges come from.
  • Watching the exam. Awake patients can see the monitor, ask questions in real time, and hear findings directly instead of through post-sedation fog.

What are the trade-offs?

Honest ones. First, discomfort is unpredictable. Prior abdominal or pelvic surgery, a history of a difficult colonoscopy, and a long or looping colon all make an awake exam harder. In the Petrini study, 85% of men who started unsedated finished without medication, versus 67% of women, a difference attributed partly to anatomy.

Second, quality can suffer if discomfort cuts the exam short. The single-center ACG abstract found lower cecal intubation (85.5% vs 95.8%) and lower adenoma detection (21.2% vs 27.4%) in unsedated exams. That was a retrospective look at a center where unsedated exams were rare; in the community study where the option was routine and patients self-selected, cecal intubation was 99.4% unsedated versus 99.3% sedated. The lesson: outcomes are excellent when the endoscopist does awake exams regularly and the patient chose it freely.

Third, not every center offers it. Some units are built entirely around anesthesia-assisted workflows and will decline.

Can you change your mind partway through?

Yes, and this is the detail that makes trying it low-risk. Centers that offer unsedated colonoscopy place an IV line before starting, so medication is seconds away if you ask. In the Petrini study, patients who started awake and requested medication mid-procedure still had a 99.1% completion rate to the cecum. Starting awake does not lock you in; it just keeps the option of comfort open in both directions.

How do you arrange an unsedated colonoscopy?

Four steps:

  1. Ask when you schedule, not on procedure day. The center needs to plan for it, and anesthesia may already be booked by default.
  2. Ask whether the endoscopist does unsedated exams regularly, and whether they use water-assisted or CO2 insertion. Experience with awake patients is the strongest predictor of a comfortable exam.
  3. Confirm the driving and escort policy in writing. If a center requires an escort even for unsedated patients, you lose one of the main benefits.
  4. Do the prep well. A clean colon means less maneuvering and a faster, more comfortable exam. Our complete prep guide covers how.

Whatever you choose, the sedation question should not decide whether you get screened at all. A colonoscopy with deep sedation, moderate sedation, or no sedation are all the same exam where it counts. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you have a colonoscopy without any sedation?

Yes. Sedation is standard practice in the United States, but it is optional at many endoscopy centers. In a community study of 2,091 patients who were offered the choice, 27.6% started without sedation and 81.1% of them completed the exam without any medication. Ask your gastroenterologist ahead of time, because not every center offers an unsedated option.

Does an unsedated colonoscopy hurt?

Most people feel pressure and cramping rather than sharp pain, and the discomfort peaks when the scope rounds bends in the colon. Tolerance varies widely. In the same community study, 97.4% of patients who finished without sedation were satisfied with their comfort and willing to repeat the exam unsedated. Water-exchange insertion technique reduces insertion pain in randomized trials.

Are you completely asleep during a normal colonoscopy?

Not always. With moderate sedation, usually midazolam plus fentanyl, you are drowsy but can respond to voice, and many people remember little afterward. With propofol deep sedation you are typically unresponsive and remember nothing. Which one you get depends on your center, your health history, and sometimes your insurance.

Can you drive yourself home after an unsedated colonoscopy?

Often yes, and that is one of the main reasons people choose it, but confirm the policy with your endoscopy center in advance. If you receive any sedative during the procedure, the NIDDK advises that you cannot drive afterward and must arrange a ride home. Some centers require an escort for every patient regardless of sedation.

Can you change your mind partway through?

Yes. Centers that offer unsedated colonoscopy typically place an IV line first, so medication can be given at any point if you ask. In the community study, patients who requested medication mid-procedure still had a 99.1% cecal intubation rate, so switching does not compromise the exam.
