How Often Do You Need a Colonoscopy if You Have Ulcerative Colitis?

November 21, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most people with ulcerative colitis need a first surveillance colonoscopy about 8 years after symptoms start. After that, the gap is 1 to 5 years, set by your risk. Per the 2021 AGA clinical practice update, people with primary sclerosing cholangitis need a colonoscopy every year.

Why does ulcerative colitis change your colonoscopy schedule?

Long-term inflammation in the colon raises the chance of colorectal cancer. A 2022 review in Gastroenterology puts the risk at about 2 to 3 times that of people without colitis, for long-standing ulcerative colitis beyond the rectum. The newest UK guideline, from the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) in 2025, uses more recent data and puts it at 1.4 to 1.7 times.

The good news is that the risk has dropped over time. Better medicines and regular surveillance both seem to help. The NIDDK lists the factors that push risk higher:

  • Colitis that covers more of the large intestine.
  • More severe inflammation.
  • Colitis that started at a younger age or has lasted longer.
  • Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), a bile duct disease linked to colitis.
  • A family history of colorectal cancer.

Colitis-related cancer can grow from flat, hard-to-see precancerous cells called dysplasia. That is why the schedule is tighter than routine screening at 45, and why the exam looks different from a standard polyp search.

When should your first surveillance colonoscopy happen?

Most guidelines start the clock at symptom onset, not at the date of diagnosis. Many people have symptoms for months or years before a diagnosis is made.

  • Most people with UC: about 8 to 10 years after symptoms began, per the guideline summary in Gastroenterology. The 2025 BSG guideline offers a baseline exam to everyone with colitis at 8 years.
  • UC with PSC: start right away. The Gastroenterology review says screening should happen at the time of PSC diagnosis, with yearly exams after that. The BSG says yearly exams begin from the date of the colitis diagnosis.
  • The UK NICE guideline (older): NICE CG118 starts surveillance 10 years after symptoms began.

The first exam does double duty. It checks for dysplasia and it maps how much of your colon the colitis has reached. That map, plus the biopsy results, sets every interval after it.

How often do you need a colonoscopy after the first one?

US guidance comes mainly from the American Gastroenterological Association's 2021 clinical practice update, written by Murthy and colleagues. Its tiers, as summarized in the 2022 Gastroenterology review, look like this:

  • Every year: PSC, moderate or severe inflammation of any extent, a parent, sibling or child diagnosed with colorectal cancer before 50, dense pseudopolyps, or higher-risk dysplasia within the past 5 years.
  • Every 2 to 3 years: mild inflammation, a strong family history (but no first-degree relative diagnosed before 50), signs of past severe colitis such as moderate pseudopolyps or a lot of scarring, or older or lower-risk dysplasia.
  • Every 5 years: continuous remission since the last exam with a healed lining on the current exam, plus either 2 or more exams in a row with no dysplasia or colitis that never spread far.

Other groups land in the same range. The NIDDK says doctors typically recommend a colonoscopy every 1 to 3 years, starting 8 years after UC began. A review in American Family Physician gives the same 1 to 3 year range, with yearly exams for PSC. NICE uses 1, 3 or 5 years for high, intermediate and low risk.

The 2025 BSG guideline goes the other way on the longest gap. It dropped its old 5-year interval and now uses 1 or 3 years for people who need ongoing surveillance. It cites a Cochrane review: surveillance every 3 years or more often cut the risk of colorectal cancer by about one-third and the risk of death from it by about two-thirds, compared with longer gaps or none.

Do you need surveillance if UC is only in your rectum?

Often not. The BSG guideline notes that ulcerative proctitis does not raise colorectal cancer risk (adjusted hazard ratio 0.97). It says ongoing surveillance should not be offered to that group. NICE also excludes "proctitis alone" from its surveillance offer.

There is a catch. Colitis can spread over time. The BSG still offers everyone a baseline exam at 8 years, partly to confirm that the disease has stayed in the rectum. If a later flare looks different, ask whether your extent needs a fresh look. People with proctitis still need routine screening at the usual age.

What does a surveillance timeline look like in practice?

Here is one example, built from the AGA tiers above. Your doctor may set a different plan.

  1. Year 0: symptoms start. Diagnosis of left-sided UC comes in year 1.
  2. Year 8: first surveillance colonoscopy. The colitis now reaches past the left side. Mild inflammation is still present. No dysplasia is found. Next exam: 2 to 3 years.
  3. Year 10 or 11: second exam. The lining has healed and there is no dysplasia. Next exam: still 2 to 3 years, because the AGA's 5-year tier needs 2 clean exams in a row or limited colitis.
  4. Year 13 or so: third exam, also clean, with continuous remission. The 5-year tier may now apply.
  5. Any year: a PSC diagnosis, a flare with moderate inflammation, or dysplasia moves you to yearly exams.

Each result resets the next interval. This is a key difference from polyp-based intervals, which follow the size and type of each polyp.

How is a surveillance colonoscopy different from a regular one?

The Gastroenterology review says the exam should be done, as far as possible, while your colitis is in remission. Active inflammation can look like dysplasia and hide real lesions. The AAFP review gives the same advice.

  • High-definition scopes are standard. Some doctors add chromoendoscopy, which uses dye sprayed on the lining (or a light filter) to make flat lesions stand out. The BSG favors pan-colonic dye spray; NICE also calls for chromoscopy.
  • Biopsies are targeted. Any abnormal area is sampled. Some doctors also take random biopsies, especially in higher-risk patients.
  • Clean prep matters more. Flat dysplasia is easy to miss in a poorly cleaned colon.
  • Experience matters. The review advises endoscopists with experience in colitis-related dysplasia.

Waiting for biopsy results can be stressful. If that worry builds between exams, anxiety.md covers ways to manage health-related anxiety.

Before each exam, ask your gastroenterologist these questions:

  1. How far did my colitis reach at its worst?
  2. Was there any inflammation or dysplasia last time?
  3. Do I have PSC, strictures or pseudopolyps?
  4. Will you use dye spray or virtual chromoendoscopy?
  5. When is my next exam due, and what would change that?

Pseudopolyps (also called post-inflammatory polyps) are scars from past inflammation, not true adenomatous polyps. They still count as a risk marker.

The bottom line

If you have ulcerative colitis beyond the rectum, plan on a first surveillance colonoscopy about 8 years after symptoms began. After that, expect an exam every 1 to 3 years, with a 5-year gap possible in long, deep remission under US guidance. PSC means yearly exams from the start. Ask your doctor to write down your next due date and the reason for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Crohn's disease follow the same colonoscopy schedule as ulcerative colitis?

Crohn's disease that involves the colon is usually handled the same way. Most guidelines group ulcerative colitis and Crohn's colitis together. Crohn's limited to the end of the small intestine does not carry the same colon cancer risk, per the 2025 BSG guideline.

Can a stool test or Cologuard replace surveillance colonoscopy in UC?

No. Surveillance in colitis looks for flat dysplasia and needs biopsies, which only a colonoscopy can provide. Guidelines for colitis surveillance are built around colonoscopy, not stool tests.

Should I schedule my surveillance colonoscopy during a flare?

Ask your doctor first. Active inflammation can make dysplasia harder to see, so doctors often prefer to do the exam when colitis is quiet. If a flare lines up with your due date, ask your doctor whether to treat the flare first.

Do I still need surveillance after my colon is removed?

It depends on the surgery. The 2025 BSG guideline still advises regular checks of an ileoanal pouch or a retained rectum in some people, such as those with PSC or prior dysplasia. Ask your surgeon which follow-up applies to you.

What happens if dysplasia is found?

Visible dysplasia can often be removed during the colonoscopy. After that, the AGA tiers set the next exam: every year after higher-risk dysplasia, and every 2 to 3 years after older or lower-risk dysplasia. Dysplasia that cannot be removed, or that is widespread, may lead to a talk about surgery.
