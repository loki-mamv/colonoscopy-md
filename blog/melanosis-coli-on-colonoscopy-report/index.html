What Does Melanosis Coli Mean on a Colonoscopy Report?

The short answer: Melanosis coli means the lining of your colon has turned brown or black from a pigment, most often after months of senna-type (anthraquinone) laxatives. It causes no symptoms and usually fades once those laxatives stop. A 2021 meta-analysis found more polyps, but no higher colorectal cancer rate, in people with it.

What is melanosis coli, and why is the colon brown?

Melanosis coli is a dark brown or black color in the inner lining of the colon. Your doctor sees it on the camera during the colonoscopy. A biopsy can confirm it, but it is often named from the look alone. A 2020 review in Gastroenterología y Hepatología calls it a common, reversible condition with no specific symptoms.

The name is misleading. The pigment is not melanin, the pigment in skin. A 2024 case report and literature summary explains the steps:

  • Stimulant laxatives damage some cells in the colon lining, and those cells die off (apoptosis).
  • Immune cells called macrophages eat the dead cells.
  • Inside the macrophages, the debris turns into a brown pigment called lipofuscin.
  • The pigment builds up in the tissue just under the surface, so the lining looks brown.

The same summary says it is most often seen in the right (proximal) colon, in people over 60, and more often in women.

Which laxatives and herbs cause melanosis coli?

The main cause is regular use of anthraquinone (anthranoid) laxatives. The 2020 review says most studies show a significant link between these laxatives and melanosis coli. In a Japanese study of 690 people, 50.9% of those with melanosis coli used anthranoids regularly, compared with 6.5% of those without it.

Common sources include:

  • Senna (sennosides). This is an FDA-approved over-the-counter stimulant laxative, per MedlinePlus.
  • Cascara sagrada and aloe. The FDA ruled in 2002 that these are no longer allowed as active ingredients in over-the-counter laxative drugs, per 21 CFR 310.545. They still turn up in some herbal products.
  • Herbal teas and remedies. A 2023 review lists 20 anthraquinone-containing herbs, including rhubarb root and aloe, that may cause melanosis coli.

The 2024 case summary notes the dark color may appear 4 to 12 months after regular laxative use starts. If you drink herbal laxative or "detox" teas, check the label for senna, cascara, aloe or rhubarb.

Does melanosis coli raise your cancer risk?

The current evidence says no higher colorectal cancer risk, though more polyps are found. Here are the numbers from four studies:

  • Abu Baker 2018 (718 patients, 2,154 controls): polyps found in 33.4% with melanosis coli vs 21.8% without. Colorectal cancer was found in 0.3% vs 3.9%.
  • Wang 2023 (2,150 patients, 39,630 controls): adenoma detection rate 30.34% vs 23.92%. The authors concluded melanosis coli may not be a precancerous lesion.
  • Liu 2017 (219 patients, 438 controls): low-grade adenomas in 31.1% vs 23.3%. There was no difference in high-grade adenomas or cancer, but too few cases to be firm.
  • Katsumata 2021 meta-analysis of five studies: more hyperplastic polyps and adenomas, but no significant difference in adenocarcinoma.

Not everyone calls it harmless. Liu and colleagues also found small ulcers at the end of the small intestine in 8.0% of people with melanosis coli vs 0% of controls. They suggest it may be a sign of chronic injury to the bowel lining. So the fair summary is this: melanosis coli itself is not cancer, and it has not been shown to cause cancer, but it is a marker of long-term laxative use.

Why do doctors find more polyps in a dark colon?

There are two possible reasons, and research has not settled which one matters more.

  • Contrast effect. Pale polyps stand out against a brown background. Abu Baker and colleagues compared it to the dye sprays doctors use to spot polyps.
  • Real increase. The repeated cell damage from laxatives may itself favor polyp growth. Liu and colleagues lean toward this view.

Either way, a found polyp is a removed polyp. If polyps were taken out during your exam, your next exam date depends on their number, size and type, not on the pigment. See our guides on colon polyps and the colonoscopy interval after polyps. The Wang 2023 study above measured adenoma detection rate, the share of exams in which a doctor finds at least one adenoma.

How long does melanosis coli take to fade after stopping laxatives?

Treatment is to stop the anthraquinone laxative. No drug or procedure is needed, and the 2020 review says medical or surgical treatment should not be considered without a serious cancer. Published timelines vary:

  1. Months 4 to 12 of regular use: the pigment can first appear, per the 2024 case report.
  2. Day you stop: the cell damage stops, and macrophages begin to break down the stored pigment.
  3. About 7 months later: in that case report, an 81-year-old woman who had taken senna for 5 years had a fully clear colon on repeat colonoscopy.
  4. Up to about 1 year: the typical outer range the same paper cites for the dark color to resolve. One study it cites gave at least 2 years, so slow fading is not a warning sign.

You do not need a repeat colonoscopy just to check the color. Follow the interval your doctor set for screening or polyps.

What should you ask your doctor about melanosis coli?

Bring a list of every laxative, tea and supplement you take, with how long you have used each. Then ask:

  • Was a biopsy taken, or was melanosis coli named from the look alone?
  • Were any polyps removed, and what type were they?
  • When is my next colonoscopy due, and does this finding change that date?
  • Should I stop senna or my herbal laxative, and what should I use instead?
  • Is something else causing my constipation, such as a medicine or another health condition?

Do not just stop a laxative you have used for years on your own. The 2024 case report notes rebound constipation can follow; in that case the doctors switched the patient to polyethylene glycol. NIDDK advises using stimulant laxatives only if constipation is severe or other treatments have failed. It also says to talk with your doctor if you cannot have a bowel movement without a laxative. MedlinePlus says senna should not be used for more than 1 week without a provider's supervision. The bottom line

Melanosis coli is a harmless-looking brown stain in the colon lining, made of lipofuscin from cells damaged by senna-type laxatives. Large studies link it to more polyps found, but not to more colorectal cancer. It usually fades within about a year of stopping the laxative. The real follow-up items are your polyp results, your next exam date, and a safer long-term plan for constipation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is melanosis coli the same as melanoma?

No. Melanoma is a cancer of pigment cells, while melanosis coli is a stain from lipofuscin inside immune cells in the colon lining. The similar names come from the dark color only. Melanosis coli is not a cancer.

Can melanosis coli cause symptoms like pain or bleeding?

Melanosis coli itself causes no symptoms. Any pain, bleeding or change in bowel habits has another cause and should be checked. The constipation that led to laxative use is often the main ongoing problem.

Do MiraLAX or stool softeners cause melanosis coli?

Studies tie melanosis coli to anthraquinone laxatives such as senna, cascara, aloe and rhubarb. Polyethylene glycol (MiraLAX) is an osmotic laxative, not an anthraquinone. In one 2024 case report, doctors switched a patient from senna to polyethylene glycol and the pigment cleared.

Can the bowel prep for my colonoscopy cause melanosis coli?

No. The pigment builds up over months of regular laxative use, not from a single prep. One 2024 case report summary says it can appear after 4 to 12 months of regular use.

Will I need colonoscopies more often because of melanosis coli?

Not because of the pigment alone. Your next exam date depends on your age, family history and any polyps that were removed. Ask your doctor whether your polyp findings change the usual interval.
